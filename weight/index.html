Weight

Dave Power·November 2026·21 minute read

The question

I started writing this on holiday, because of my nephew. He is eleven, he comes from a farming family, and he is more cheeky than inquisitive. Somewhere over the week he worked out that I work with AI, which to him meant I was helping it. As far as he is concerned AI is the greatest evil in the world. It is going to take everybody’s jobs and it is going to destroy farming. So he asked me, with some relish, why I was helping it.

I did not have a good answer. I had the kind of answers you give on a conference panel, and none of them would have lasted a minute with an eleven-year-old who wanted to know why his uncle was on the wrong side. He had me, he knew it, and he enjoyed it enormously.

It has bothered me ever since. I have spent twenty years working with technology and the people who build it, and it took a child on his holidays to make me say plainly why. I could not answer him because I was not sure myself. I did not know whether AI is a force for good, and I did not know where it stops and we begin. Until I had worked that out, anything I told him would have been another panel answer.

So I went looking, which is why this essay takes the long way round to his answer. Over the last few weeks I have read more than I expected to, gone back to things I half remembered, and asked a great many questions, a lot of them of the very AI I was trying to justify, which I accept is a bit like asking the accused to help with the investigation. Most of what follows I did not know in September.

Here is where I ended up. Everything humanity has ever needed and written down is a kind of list, and the machine can work through that list faster than we can. But the needs that matter most are the ones that are not on it yet, because they only appear the first time someone stands somewhere new. And the machine cannot feel the weight of any of them, which turned out to matter more than I expected. The rest of this essay is how I got there, and what it means for the people at the bottom of the ladder, which is where my nephew’s generation will be in ten years.

What the machine can do

The thing my nephew is afraid of happened to me once, in a small way. I studied industrial design in college. The day the design lab got its first 3D modelling software, a package called SolidWorks, I stood behind a first-year student and watched him rotate a perfect assembly drawing on the screen. He wasn’t gloating. I don’t think he knew what he had. I knew, because I was two years into the course and had spent those two years at a drawing board with ink and a ruler, learning to do what he had just done in an afternoon. Somewhere in those two years I had decided that being good at it was what made me a designer.

That was the end of the nineties. I was good at sketching, inking and rendering, and I had the grades to prove it. Then a piece of software arrived and within a term the gap between me and the bottom of the class was gone. I felt cheated, and I am sorry to say I sulked about it for the best part of a year. What I could not see was that the drawing was only ever the part of the job a lecturer could mark. What mattered was the product. The course moved on to the quality of our ideas, which was exactly what it should have done, and I held it against them anyway.

What happened to me in that design lab is now happening to a lot of people at once. If most of your job can be checked and marked, there is a good chance a machine can now do a fair bit of it. For a while I comforted myself that there was one thing it still could not do, which was have an original idea.

Then I got talking to Tim at the AI summit in Dublin. Tim came through Patch, the accelerator for gifted kids at Dogpatch Labs, where I used to work. Time in a room with them is the quickest way I know to feel stupid, and Tim is a little older now, which has not helped. His proof that machines can be creative, that they can originate, was that in May an OpenAI model disproved a conjecture Paul Erdős posed in 1946, one that mathematicians had been stuck on for eighty years.1 He shook my faith in humans. For a bit.

Sam Altman later told the story of the weekend that model arrived. Someone set up a channel called Hold My Beer, the staff threw in the hardest problems from their own academic careers, and it knocked them over one after another. He said that alongside the excitement he felt more sadness, and a stronger sense of something ending, than he had expected.

It took me a while to see what was wrong with that, and with Tim’s proof. Look at what was on the table before the model started. Erdős left behind a list of more than a thousand problems he thought were worth solving, and this one he had carried around for fifty years because it bothered him. The machine has never been bothered by anything. It was handed his question and it answered it, brilliantly, but it was still his question.

I think Sam Altman was mourning the wrong thing, and that Tim, for once, was wrong. The original act is not solving a problem but coming up with it. Someone has to look at the world, notice what is missing, and care enough to write it down. Every problem on the machine’s list was put there by a person.

That left me with a narrower question. Why was Erdős bothered, and why is the machine not?

What it is missing

The best answer I have starts with a cough. I have had one this autumn that will not shift, and at some point I asked a model about it. The answer was correct, complete and no use to me whatsoever, because it was the answer for the average person with the average cough. When one of my children is sick the same thing happens with far more riding on it. The model can tell me everything about the illness. It has no idea how heavy it is for me on that particular night. That weight matters.

Shifting a cough and getting a sick child better are both needs, and I have come to think that every need has two parts. There is what it is, which is public and which anyone can look up. With my child, that is the illness. And there is how much it weighs, which is private, which changes by the hour, and which only the person carrying it knows. With my child, that is how worried I am that night. The machine has the first part of every need ever written down and the second part of none of them.

A neurologist has described what a mind is like when it has the first part without the second. In the 1980s Antonio Damasio treated a man he calls Elliot, who had a tumour removed from the front of his brain.2 Afterwards Elliot scored normally on every test. His intelligence, memory and reasoning were intact, and yet he could no longer run his life. He lost his job, his marriage and his savings, and when Damasio asked him how he felt about any of it, he talked as if it had all happened to someone else. He could lay out every option and argue for each one, but he could not feel which one mattered. Every option weighed the same, so he could deliberate for hours and choose nothing.

Elliot had lost the weight. The machines never had it. That is what connects him to Erdős. You need weight to choose between the options in front of you, and you need it to notice that a problem is worth having in the first place. Erdős had it, and it kept him at one question for fifty years.

So where does weight come from? I think it starts with having a body. We get hungry and cold and tired, we get sick, we get old, and we know we are going to die. Everything we care about grows out of that, from the obvious things like food and shelter to the less obvious ones like wanting to be remembered. Those needs are not a layer on top of who we are. They are who we are. Put plainly, we have something to lose, and a thing weighs on us according to how much of it is at risk. That is why my cough is a nuisance and a sick child is something else entirely.

The machine has none of this. It does not get hungry, it does not get older, and nothing happens to it when it is wrong. I have a stake in my child getting better, and the model has none.

Why the list is never finished

If weight comes from having something to lose, then a new need can only be found by someone with something to lose. The machine can learn what we need by reading about it, but it cannot have a need of its own, and it cannot discover a new one, because discovering a need means being somewhere, with something to lose, and finding out what that place does to you.

Let me give you an example. I am a bit of a sci-fi nerd, so bear with me while I talk about Mars for a minute.

Nobody will cross the room to watch a robot land on Mars. We have landed several and most people could not name one. When a person first steps onto Mars, a billion people will be watching, because every one of them will be in the suit. Someone with the same body and the same fears will have done a thing nobody has done, and for a moment everyone watching will believe that people like us can do more than we thought. Nobody gets that from a robot.

And that is just the landing. The day after, when a person has to live there, needs appear that nobody has ever had, and none of them are in any book because nobody has been there. The machine can only learn needs that have already been recorded. It is always one step behind the first person to stand somewhere new.

Mars is an extreme case, but you do not have to leave the planet to stand somewhere new, because society keeps moving under our feet. Having a child is the oldest thing there is, and yet what it takes to raise one, how childcare works, what it costs, who is around to help, how much time two working parents actually have, is different from what it was ten years ago, and it will be different again in ten more. The needs change because the world around them changes. Human needs are not a fixed quantity that the machine can work through. They are made fresh every time the ground shifts, and the frontier is not forty million miles away.

There is a reason nobody can anticipate those needs in advance, and it is not a lack of intelligence. We are decent at seeing one step ahead. Past that, every step branches, and the number of things that could happen grows faster than anyone can think. Rockets are a good example, and yes, I am back on space again. For fifty years we tried to anticipate everything that could go wrong with a rocket before it flew, and the result was rockets that were not very reliable. SpaceX builds one, flies it, watches it blow up, and learns what the list was missing. That approach produced the Falcon 9, which has flown more than seven hundred times and is the most reliable rocket ever built. The best engineers on the planet, with the best AI on the planet, are still learning by doing, because reality holds information nobody has yet and the only way to get it is to go and be there. A model is the old approach taken to its limit. It is everything already written down, thinking very hard before the flight.

Our part in the machine

None of this would matter to my nephew if it stopped at needs, but a job has the same two parts as a need. Take an accountant at a hospital. There is the accounting, which can be checked and marked. And there is what the accounting is for, which is a hospital that works, and the hospital is for the child in the bed. All the weight is at that end of the chain, and the accountant is a long way from it.

I think we have been very caught up in our part in the machine. We say I am a designer, I am a chemist, I am an accountant, and we make the part into who we are. I did it with a drawing board, and I took it badly when the part was taken away. But the part was never the point. What mattered about my drawings was the product, and what matters about the accounts is the hospital.

This changed what I think the machine is doing. It is taking the half of the job that can be marked, which happens to be the half we named ourselves after. It cannot take the other half, because the other half is knowing what the work is for and caring whether it gets there, and that needs something to lose. Isn’t that better? I think it might be, on two conditions. There has to be enough work in the second half, and people have to be able to get to it.

What is left to do

My nephew’s fear, and most people’s, is that the machine takes the jobs and nothing replaces them. I think that fear comes from asking the question the wrong way round. We look at the jobs that exist today, the designers and engineers and chemists, and we ask which ones the machine will take. In October Andreessen Horowitz published a map of where consumer AI has room to grow.3 It shows the categories we already have, such as travel, retail, dating and personal finance, where no AI product has yet taken on the incumbents. I found it disappointing, because it makes the same mistake. The companies of the future will not be AI versions of the ones we have now. They will be built around needs that are not on anyone’s list yet.

The case for plenty of work is simple enough to state. Work is what people do to meet needs. A machine makes it cheaper to meet the needs already on the list, and every time that has happened before, people have moved on to needs that were not worth anyone’s time until then. Two hundred years ago most people worked the land. Then came the machines my nephew’s family now depend on, and the people who left the land did not sit idle. A study led by David Autor at MIT found that about six in ten jobs in America in 2018 were of a kind that did not exist in 1940.4 Nobody in 1940 could have listed them.

I cannot list the next ones either, and I would distrust anyone who says they can. What I can say is that we are nowhere near the end of what people need. Nobody has cured the common cold, which I mention only because I still have mine. Nobody has worked out how two working parents are supposed to raise a child either. Follow the accountant’s chain past the hospital and you reach cancer, sickle cell and Crohn’s disease, and nobody there is short of work. And those are needs we already know about. Every problem the machine solves puts people somewhere new, and somewhere new has needs nobody has catalogued. Until every human need is met there is no limit to the work to be done, and we are a very long way from that.

That is an argument that the work will exist. It does not say the change will be gentle, or that the people who lose the old work will be the ones who get the new. And the new work has one awkward property, which is that nobody can do it from the list. Somebody has to stand in the new place and decide what is needed, and that takes judgement.

How judgement is learned

Having a body gives everyone a stake in their own life. It does not give anyone judgement in a field. The philosopher Brian Cantwell Smith says that judgement is not a better kind of calculation but calculation done by something that can be hurt by the result.5 I think that is right, and I think it has to be learned. I got mine, such as it is, at the drawing board. The finished drawings I was so proud of turned out to be worthless, but the sketching was not. A sketch is thinking on paper. You draw the thing, see that a person would not want to hold it, and draw it again. Each version is a guess at a need you cannot fully state, and after two years and thousands of bad guesses I could tell a good one from a bad one. The design course understood that. When SolidWorks arrived it dropped the finished drawings and kept the sketching, and designers are still taught to sketch today. The software took the output and left the thinking alone.

I learned it a second time as a founder. Hiri was an email company I co-founded, and when we started there were no users, so there was nothing to measure, so every decision was a guess. Most were small, like what should happen when a message fails to send, whether a feature nobody has asked for is worth a week, and which of two complaints is the real one. There were dozens of those a day for years, and not one of them had data behind it. None of them could be checked and marked either, because there was no right answer to check against. Earlier I said a machine can now do a fair bit of any job that can be checked and marked. This was the opposite kind of work, and I do not think AI could have done it.

One guess along the way was to build for people running Microsoft Exchange on Linux, which is every bit as niche as it sounds. There was no data on those people because nobody had thought them worth counting. Years later Hiri was the most popular Exchange client on Linux, which is not where we started and not where we were aiming. A long and winding path took us there, one decision at a time, and if you had asked me on day one what the company would become I would have told you with great confidence and been wrong. That is what a founder does all day. You stand somewhere nobody has stood, with no data, and you decide. I have watched hundreds of startups do the same at Wayra, which is Telefónica’s accelerator, and at Dogpatch, where I ran the 2050 Accelerator. Deciding without data is a skill, and you get it the way I got mine at the drawing board, by doing it badly first, with something riding on the result, until you can tell a good call from a bad one before the evidence arrives. That is what I mean by judgement.

So judgement is not something you are born with, and it is not something you can read. You get it by carrying the weight of your own decisions for long enough. What worries me is where the next lot of people are going to get the chance.

What we are taking apart

The design course kept the sketching. Most firms adopting AI are doing the reverse. Look at what is being automated first: the junior analyst, the junior accountant, the first-year associate, the graduate developer. Their work is the easiest to check and mark, which is why the machines take it first, but those are also the jobs where people first find out what the work is for, and first carry the weight of their own decisions. Stanford’s payroll data from late 2025 shows workers aged 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI losing around 13 percent of their employment relative to everyone else, while older workers in the same jobs held steady.6 Two researchers at Anthropic ran a trial this year in which developers learned a new software library with and without AI help, and the ones who used the AI understood it measurably less and were no faster.7 Matt Beane, a researcher at UC Santa Barbara, spent years watching surgical residents in American hospitals. When surgical robots arrived the senior surgeon no longer needed a resident’s hands, so the residents stood at the back of the room watching a screen.8

I was at London Tech Week in June, and when I went through the exhibitor list afterwards about a quarter of the AI stands were selling workflow automation and another fifth were consultancies selling the same thing as a service. Firms are buying automation of routine work, and routine work is where the apprentices were. The CTOs on stage said they were not seeing productivity gains. Even if they got every saving they hoped for, they would be spending the next generation’s judgement to get it.

Finance is no better. For thirty years it has treated the measurable middle, the analysis and the operations and the compliance checks, as the business. What a financial firm actually has is a regulated licence at the bottom and a relationship with a worried person at the top. The people who can hold that relationship learned how in the middle, doing the analysis as juniors until they understood what a client stands to lose. If the machine does all of that work, I do not know where their successors will learn it.

A firm that stops hiring juniors because the machine does their work will have nobody qualified to sign off on anything in fifteen years. Worse than that, it will have nobody who can notice a need before it is written down, because that is what the juniors were learning all along, without anyone calling it that. In freeing ourselves from the work the machine can do, we are getting rid of the one thing it cannot replace.

What I would ask

If all of this is right, then the question about AI is not whether there will be work for people. There will be more than we can do. The question is who gets the chance to learn judgement the hard way, and who decides what the machine is pointed at. Left alone, it gets pointed at cost, because a saving is the easiest thing to measure. Nobody sets out to remove the bottom of the ladder. It happens because the people deciding already have their judgement, and the young people who stand to lose are not in the room.

The one thing I would ask of any firm, including my own, is to rebuild the apprenticeship on purpose, because it will not survive on its own. My design course showed how. It gave the drawings to the software and kept the sketching. A firm can give the marked work to the machine and keep its juniors on the decisions nobody can mark. The best place I know to do it is wherever there is no data yet, which means the startup, the cohort and the proof of concept with no users. I have been running those for a decade under the name of accelerators, and it is a little embarrassing to admit that I never noticed this was what they were for. They are one of the few places left where a young person has to decide without a mark and then live with the result.

I worry even more about school. The things that will matter are the things school is worst at teaching. One is sketching, in the broad sense of thinking by trying. Another is judgement, which is knowing a good guess from a bad one before the evidence arrives. The third is noticing what is needed before anyone has written it down. Demis Hassabis, who runs DeepMind, has argued that the classroom has to be turned inside out, with the rote learning handed to the machine and the time in the room given over to working on problems together. I think he is right, and I see little sign of it happening. Across the country teachers are still preparing the same lessons in isolation, each one alone with the same curriculum. That is madness. It is exactly the work the machine should be doing for them, so that they can do what only a teacher can.

The answer

So, to my nephew. You asked why I help it. Here is the best I can do.

You are right that it is coming for a lot of jobs, and right that it is coming quickly. Like the printing press and the tractor before it, it will hurt people on its way through, and the young first. I have seen this go wrong before. I once believed social media would be a force for good, and we let it run amok. This will arrive whether anyone helps or not, so what matters is that we shape it. That is why I help it.

I also believe that people should keep progressing. I am a bit of a Trekkie, and like Gene Roddenberry I think people get better over time. I have some evidence for it. I was a scout leader for years, and every intake of kids was wiser and kinder than the one before, screens and all. I was a little shit by comparison. So it is not AI that I support. What I support is better, and what comes next.

And this is an incredibly powerful tool. It takes the known work off our hands so that we can spend our time at the edge, where the new problems are. It gives ordinary people knowledge and expertise they could never get at before. Just imagine what that could do for farming, for our environment and for feeding the world.

As for the farm, you love it. Anyone can see that the romance of it has you. I know that feeling. I had it the first time I saw Mars through a telescope, and again the first time I played with a computer, a Commodore 16 that my uncle gave to me. The machine can tell you everything about Mars and everything about the farm, and it will never once want to be on either of them. That wanting is the weight I have been writing about, and you already have it. There will always be room for romance.

You will learn the judgement too, because your apprenticeship is the farm, your way of life. So here is some advice from the uncle you beat. People are going to ask what you want to be when you grow up. I said a designer, and a piece of software took the part of that I was proudest of. So do not tell them what you want to be. Tell them what you want to work on, because nobody can take a problem off you. Farming is not solved. No farmer has ever stood where you will be standing in ten years, and you will have a tool none of them had. My uncle handed me mine. Now I am the uncle. Use it. Don’t simply farm. Solve for the effects of climate change. Solve degrading soils. Solve hunger. Be better than your father. Be better than your uncle. AI is not the frontier. You are.
